# Alignment Explanation: Slides, Content, and Lesson Plan

## Why Conditionals?

I chose conditionals as the topic for the first class because they are the earliest point in programming where students must think algorithmically rather than procedurally. Writing a sequence of print statements requires no decision-making; writing an `if`/`else` statement requires a student to model a branching reality. This shift in thinking is foundational — loops, functions, and data structures all depend on it. Conditionals also connect immediately to students' lived experience: every choice they make daily maps cleanly onto an `if`/`else` structure, which makes the concept accessible before any syntax is introduced.

## How the Slides Align with the Lesson Plan

The slide deck is structured to mirror the lesson plan section by section, so that each slide serves a specific pedagogical purpose at a specific moment in the 50-minute class.

Slides 1 through 3 (title, roadmap, objectives) open the class by orienting students. Displaying the roadmap and five SWBAT objectives gives students a cognitive map of the lesson, reducing extraneous load during new instruction.

Slides 4 and 5 correspond to the 5-minute Hook. Rather than defining conditionals abstractly, slide 4 presents a familiar real-world scenario — umbrella or sunscreen — rendered as a flowchart. This activates prior knowledge before any Python appears. Slide 5 then formalizes the vocabulary (condition, Boolean, branch) without code, so students have language for what they already understand intuitively.

Slides 6 through 10 map to the 12-minute Direct Instruction phase. Each slide introduces exactly one new element: `if` syntax, comparison operators, `else`, `elif` in the abstract, and `elif` in a concrete grade-classifier example. The deliberate one-concept-per-slide sequencing prevents students from being asked to process syntax, semantics, and a new operator simultaneously.

Slides 11 and 12 correspond to Guided Practice (17–27 min). Both are live-coding slides — students type alongside the instructor. The Temperature Advisor uses `elif` in a meaningful context; the Even/Odd example introduces the modulo operator cleanly. Both slides prompt students to predict output before running, which is the "tracing" skill listed in objective 5.

Slides 13 and 14 support the Main Activity (27–42 min). Slide 13 presents the Day Planner task with a starter template to reduce blank-page anxiety. Slide 14 offers tiered extensions so advanced students are challenged without the instructor needing to differentiate verbally mid-class.

Slides 15 and 16 address the two most common student errors — `=` vs. `==` and indentation — and model the tracing process. These reinforce the closing debrief planned for 42–47 min.

Slides 17 through 20 cover the Closing, Summary, Homework, and Preview, matching the final 8 minutes of the lesson plan exactly.
